Motion design is the part of a project that quietly eats the calendar. The edit is locked, the client loves it, and then someone asks for an animated logo sting, kinetic captions, a product callout and three aspect ratios by Thursday. Your one motion designer is already on two other jobs.

Outsourcing motion design solves the capacity problem fast. It also creates a new one if you do it carelessly: work that looks like it came from a different studio. This guide covers how to outsource motion design so the output still looks like yours.

## Why motion quality slips when you outsource

Most outsourced motion goes wrong for the same few reasons, and none of them are about talent.

- **No motion language.** Brands have colour and type rules, but almost nobody writes down how things move. Easing, speed, how text enters, how long a logo holds. A new designer fills that gap with their own taste.

- **Feedback through three people.** The client tells the account lead, who tells the producer, who paraphrases it to the animator. "Make it pop" survives that chain. Specifics do not.

- **Files that do not travel.** Missing fonts, linked assets on someone's desktop, plugins the partner does not own. The first day of every job goes on rebuilding.

- **No one owns the final look.** If the partner delivers and nobody senior checks it against the brand before it goes out, small inconsistencies stack up over a month of content.

Fix those four and most of the quality risk goes away.

## What to outsource first

Not every motion job is equally safe to hand over. Start with work that has clear rules and a lot of volume, then move to the craft heavy pieces once the partner knows your style.

- **Good first jobs:** caption styling and kinetic text for reels, lower thirds and title cards, resizing and reformatting existing animations, end cards and CTA frames for ads.

- **Second wave:** logo animations, product callouts over live footage, animated social posts, explainer sections inside a longer film.

- **Keep close until trust is built:** hero brand films, full 3D product animation, anything that defines a new campaign look.

This order lets you test a partner on jobs where mistakes are cheap and visible, before you trust them with the work your client will remember.

## Build a motion style sheet before the first brief

A one page motion style sheet does more for quality than any contract clause. It should answer the questions a new designer would otherwise guess at.

- **Easing and pace:** do things snap, glide or bounce? Give a reference clip, not an adjective.

- **Type behaviour:** how headlines and captions enter and leave, safe margins, maximum words on screen.

- **Logo rules:** the approved animated logo, how long it holds, and where it may appear.

- **Colour and texture:** brand colours in RGB and hex, whether gradients, grain or shadows are allowed.

- **Sound:** whether motion is cut to music, has its own sound design, or must work silent with captions.

- **Formats:** the aspect ratios, frame rates and export settings you deliver every time.

Attach two or three past pieces that you consider exactly right, and one that missed and why. That last one is often the most useful page in the pack.

## How to brief an outsourced motion designer

A good motion brief is short and concrete. If you already brief live action well, the same habits apply. Our guide on [how to brief a product video](/journal/how-to-brief-a-product-video) covers the basics, and motion adds a few of its own.

- **The job in one line:** what this piece is for and where it will run.

- **The locked edit or storyboard:** with timecodes for every moment that needs motion.

- **Copy, final:** every word that appears on screen, approved, in a text file. Retyping copy from a screenshot is where typos are born.

- **Assets:** logos as vectors, fonts with licences, project files, product renders or footage, all in one shared folder.

- **References:** one or two clips with a note on what to take from each, so the designer copies the timing, not the whole idea.

- **Deadline and review points:** when you want a first pass, when final is due, and who signs off.

Ask for a short style frame or a five second test before the full animation. It costs a few hours and saves days of rework if the direction is off.

## Set up review so feedback lands

Most quality problems are really feedback problems. A few habits keep revisions tight.

- Give timecoded comments on the actual video, not a paragraph in an email. Frame accurate review tools make this easy.

- Collect all feedback from your side and the client's into one round. Two people giving opposite notes on the same version wastes a cycle.

- Name one person on your team who makes the final call on look and feel. Not a committee.

- Agree the number of revision rounds up front, and what counts as a revision versus a new request.

For agencies, the rule is simple: the partner talks to you, you talk to the client. That keeps the relationship and the creative direction in one place. We explain the full setup in our guide to [white label video editing for agencies](/journal/white-label-video-editing-for-agencies).

## What to check before you choose a partner

The first page of search results for this topic is mostly vendor pages listing tools and benefits. Tools are not the question. Almost every serious studio uses After Effects, Cinema 4D or Blender. What separates partners is how they work.

- **Relevant reel:** ask for motion work in your category, not their best piece overall. Beauty motion and fintech explainers are different skills.

- **A paid test:** one real job, paid, with your brief and your feedback loop. You learn more in a week than from any call.

- **Who does the work:** will the same designer or small team handle your jobs each time, or whoever is free?

- **File handover:** do you receive clean, organised project files at the end, so you are never locked in?

- **Rights and confidentiality:** an NDA as standard, and clear wording that final files and project files belong to you or your client once paid.

- **Time zone fit:** decide whether you want overlap for live calls or an overnight handoff where briefs sent in your evening are back by morning. Both work. Mixing them by accident does not.

- **Capacity:** can they handle a busy month without the quality dropping, and how much notice do they need?

## Keep quality consistent over months

Getting the first job right is the easy part. Staying consistent across fifty jobs takes a little structure.

- **Templates:** once a lower third, caption style or end card is approved, have the partner build it as a reusable template. Every future job starts from the approved version.

- **A shared asset library:** one folder of logos, fonts, sound effects and approved animations that both teams use.

- **A monthly look back:** pull the month's output into one timeline and watch it together. Drift is obvious when pieces sit side by side.

- **Update the style sheet:** every time a piece of feedback repeats, it belongs in the style sheet, not in the next brief.
